At a glance

  1. There are two horizons in a diffusion policy: environment time \(t\) and denoising time \(k=K\dots0\). When comparing these methods, I mainly look at where they define the action, where they compute likelihood, and how they assign advantage to each denoising step.
  2. DDPO / DPOK treat the denoising process as one MDP. Each \(\pi_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)\) is Gaussian, so we can compute its log probability directly. Reward only appears at \(x_0\), and every step gets the same terminal reward.
  3. DPPO puts the denoising MDP inside the environment MDP, then flattens \((t,k)\) into one long chain. Reward appears at \(k=0\), after we have a clean action. The critic is \(V(s_t)\), and GAE runs at the environment level. All denoising steps for one environment decision use the same \(A_t\), multiplied by \(\gamma_{\text{denoise}}^k\). Here we use the destination index for each transition.
  4. The PPO ratio and clipping are evaluated separately at each \((t,k)\). The gradient passes through the current \(\mu_\theta(a^{k+1},s_t,k)\), not backward through the sampled denoising chain. This score-function estimator differs from the pathwise gradients in DRaFT / AlignProp.
  5. For a deterministic flow / ODE sampler, the conditional transition is a Dirac distribution. We cannot plug it into a Gaussian PPO ratio. The final output can still be random because the initial noise is random. Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO deal with this by constructing an SDE; ReinFlow and πRL Flow-Noise add learned noise. DDIM with \(\eta=0\) has the same problem at each transition.
  6. There are also methods that do not use chain log probabilities: DSRL / Noise PPO learn over initial noise, FPO builds a surrogate ratio from flow-matching loss differences, and DiffusionNFT regresses positive and negative implicit policies.

1Method genealogy

2Two-level MDP derivation: two horizons

Environment time is \(t=0,1,\dots\); denoising time is \(k=K,\dots,1,0\), from pure noise to clean sample/action. Terminal-reward image generation has one denoising horizon conditioned on prompt \(c\). In robotics, the condition is observation \(s_t\), and each environment decision generates an entire chain to obtain \(a_t^0\).

2.1A single denoising MDP: DDPO / DPOK

DDPO (Black et al., 2023) and DPOK (Fan et al., 2023) view one generation as a \(K\)-step episode:

\[ \mathbf s_k=(c,\,k,\,x_k),\qquad \mathbf a_k=x_{k-1},\qquad \pi_\theta(\mathbf a_k\mid \mathbf s_k)=\mathcal N\!\big(x_{k-1};\ \mu_\theta(x_k,k,c),\ \sigma_k^2 I\big), \] \[ P(\mathbf s_{k-1}\mid \mathbf s_k,\mathbf a_k)=\delta_{(c,\,k-1,\,\mathbf a_k)},\qquad R(\mathbf s_k,\mathbf a_k)=\begin{cases} r(x_0,c) & k=1\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \]

The MDP transition is deterministic: the next state contains the \(x_{k-1}\) we just sampled. The randomness comes from the policy. Since each policy is diagonal Gaussian, we can compute its log probability:

\[ \log\pi_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c) = -\frac{\lVert x_{k-1}-\mu_\theta(x_k,k,c)\rVert^2}{2\sigma_k^2} + \text{const}. \]

Intermediate rewards are zero, and the inner discount is one. Every transition therefore has return \(r(x_0,c)\). DDPO has two estimators:

\[ \text{DDPO}_{\text{SF}}:\ \ \nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E\Big[\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)\;r(x_0,c)\Big] \] \[ \text{DDPO}_{\text{IS}}:\ \ J=\mathbb E\Big[\textstyle\sum_{k}\min\big(\rho_k\hat A,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_k,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A\big)\Big],\quad \rho_k=\frac{\pi_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)} \]

For \(\hat A\), we can normalize rewards using a running mean and standard deviation for each prompt. This gives us a baseline without training a critic. The idea is related to GRPO, although GRPO computes its statistics from samples in the same batch. DPOK adds a KL term to the same MDP. If the initial-noise distribution is unchanged, the chain-rule bound lets us replace the terminal KL with a sum of expected Gaussian transition KLs:

\[ \mathrm{KL}\big(p_\theta(x_0\mid c)\,\Vert\,p_{\text{pre}}(x_0\mid c)\big)\le \sum_{k}\mathbb E_{p_\theta}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x_k,c)\Vert\pi_{\text{pre}}(\cdot\mid x_k,c)\big)\Big] =\sum_k \mathbb E\Big[\tfrac{\lVert\mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{pre}}\rVert^2}{2\sigma_k^2}\Big]. \]

DPOK also considers a learned baseline \(V(x_k,c)\approx\mathbb E[r(x_0,c)\mid x_k]\) in its appendix: a critic over denoising states. Compare this with the environment-only critic in DPPO.

We need positive variance for the Gaussian ratio. The log density requires \(\sigma_k>0\). For DDIM, the noise is controlled by \(\eta\), with \(\sigma_k(\eta)=\eta\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\bar\alpha_{k-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_k}}\sqrt{1-\tfrac{\bar\alpha_k}{\bar\alpha_{k-1}}}\). We need positive \(\eta\) where this coefficient is nonzero, and may still need to handle the endpoints separately or add a variance floor. With \(\eta=0\), each DDIM transition is deterministic, so this local density ratio does not apply.

2.2The DPPO two-level MDP

DPPO (Ren et al., 2024) considers robots. At decision \(t\), start at \(a_t^K\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), denoise to \(a_t^0\), execute the action (often a chunk), and transition to \(s_{t+1}\). Flatten the two levels using \(\bar t(t,k)=tK+(K-k-1)\). In this subsection, \(k=K-1,\dots,0\) indexes the destination of \(a^{k+1}\to a^k\):

\[ \bar s_{\bar t(t,k)}=(s_t,\ a_t^{k+1}),\qquad \bar a_{\bar t(t,k)}=a_t^{k},\qquad \bar R_{\bar t(t,k)}=\begin{cases}0 & k\gt 0\\ R(s_t,a_t^0) & k=0\end{cases} \] \[ k\gt 0:\ (s_t,a_t^k)\ \text{deterministically becomes the next state};\qquad k=0:\ (s_{t+1},a_{t+1}^K)\sim P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t^0)\otimes\mathcal N(0,I). \]

The policy is still Gaussian: \(\pi_\theta(a_t^k\mid s_t,a_t^{k+1})=\mathcal N(a_t^k;\mu_\theta(a_t^{k+1},s_t,k),\sigma_{k+1}^2I)\). We can compute the log probability. The remaining question is how to turn the environment reward at \(k=0\) into an advantage for each \((t,k)\).

Computing and distributing advantage. DPPO uses two levels:

\[ \delta_t=r_t+\gamma_{\text{env}}V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t),\qquad A_t=\sum_{l\ge0}(\gamma_{\text{env}}\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l},\qquad \hat A_{t,k}=\gamma_{\text{denoise}}^{\,k}\,A_t . \]
  • The critic depends on \(s_t\), not the intermediate noisy action \(a_t^{k+1}\). The paper reports instability in an ablation that also conditions the value function on the noisy action.
  • We compute advantage once per environment decision. All \(k\) use the same \(A_t\), with a weight \(\gamma_{\text{denoise}}^k\) (0.99 in the configurations discussed here). In the code, this is advantages *= gamma_denoising ** (ft_denoising_steps - i - 1). The index \(i\) increases through the trainable steps, so the last step has weight one.
  • My understanding is the following. If the critic only sees \(s_t\) and the inner discount is one, the inner TD error is \(0+V(s_t)-V(s_t)=0\). We can then pass the environment advantage to the denoising steps. The extra \(\gamma_{\text{denoise}}\) downweights early, noisy steps; it does not discount physical time. This is an intuition, though. It does not prove that running GAE on an arbitrarily flattened chain gives the same estimator as the environment-level implementation.

PPO ratios and clipping. Each \((t,k)\) contributes a separate PPO sample:

\[ \rho_{t,k}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t^k\mid s_t,a_t^{k+1})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t^k\mid s_t,a_t^{k+1})},\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{PPO}}=-\,\mathbb E_{t,k}\Big[\min\big(\rho_{t,k}\hat A_{t,k},\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t,k},1-\epsilon_k,1+\epsilon_k)\hat A_{t,k}\big)\Big]. \]
  • We save \(a_t^{k+1}\) and \(a_t^k\) during rollout and treat them as constants when updating. The gradient \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\) only goes through the current \(\mu_\theta(a_t^{k+1},s_t,k)\). It does not backpropagate through the sampled denoising chain. If we batch the updates by step, we do not need to keep the whole chain’s activations in memory. We still have to store the rollout, and both storage and total computation grow with \(K\).
  • The implementation supports timestep-dependent clipping: \(\epsilon_k=\epsilon_{\text{base}}+(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\text{base}})\frac{e^{\beta\tau_k}-1}{e^{\beta}-1}\), with \(\tau_k\in[0,1]\) increasing along denoising. Later transitions can have a wider clipping interval. The cited Robomimic configuration goes from clip_ploss_coef_base=0.001 to clip_ploss_coef=0.01; Gym uses 0.01 for both, giving no schedule.

Exploration and numerical stability. Small posterior \(\sigma_k\) near the clean end gives weak exploration and makes log probability highly sensitive to small mean changes. DPPO distinguishes a sampling floor \(\sigma^{\text{exp}}_{\min}\) (0.01–0.1 across cited tasks) from a log-probability floor \(\sigma^{\text{prob}}_{\min}=0.1\). Both are 0.1 in the cited Gym / Robomimic configurations.

Only finetune the last few steps. Make a copy \(\theta_{\text{ft}}\). Use frozen \(\theta\) for the first \(K-K^{\prime}\) transitions, then the trainable copy for the last \(K^{\prime}\), which enter the PPO loss. The configurations discussed here use \(K=20,K^{\prime}=10\). Another option is roughly five stochastic DDIM steps with \(\eta=1\) during training and \(\eta=0\) for evaluation. Execute \(T_a\) primitive actions from a chunk, then replan.

2.3Other factorizations

(a) Treat the whole chain as one action. Add the transition log probabilities to get the joint log probability; multiply the ratios to get the joint ratio:

\[ \log\pi_\theta(a^{0:K}\mid s)=\log\mathcal N(a^K;0,I)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\pi_\theta(a^{k-1}\mid a^k,s),\qquad \rho^{\text{chain}}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\rho_k=\exp\Big(\sum_k\Delta\ell_k\Big). \]

The clean-action marginal ratio \(\pi_\theta(a^0)/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a^0)\) is generally intractable, but \(\rho^{\mathrm{chain}}\) is a valid weight on the extended trajectory space. At the on-policy point, the score gradients have the same form \(\mathbb E[A\sum_k\nabla\log\pi_k]\). Clipping their product and clipping individual ratios are different surrogates: many small local changes can accumulate as roughly \((1\pm\epsilon)^K\), while joint clipping can activate frequently on long chains. PPO clipping is not a hard constraint on either ratio or KL. OGPO uses the joint product.

(b) Outcome-only group comparison. Sample \(G\) chains for condition \(c\), compute \(\hat A_i=(r_i-\bar r)/(\operatorname{std}(r)+\varepsilon)\), and broadcast it through each chain before per-step ratio clipping (Flow-GRPO, DanceGRPO). No critic is needed, but the signal remains terminal rather than denoising-step-specific. SDE construction, sample grouping, fewer steps, and shared initial noise make practical differences.

(c) RL only in noise / latent space. Freeze \(f_\theta\), absorb it into the environment, and learn \(\pi_\psi(w\mid s)\) with output \(a=f_\theta(s,w)\), often using deterministic DDIM. The density belongs to the noise policy, not the denoising chain. DSRL uses off-policy SAC; Noise PPO uses a one-decision PPO formulation.

(d) Avoid chain likelihoods altogether. One family builds surrogate ratios from ELBO / flow-matching losses (FPO). Another learns through regression, including DiffusionNFT’s positive and negative implicit policies. A simpler related baseline is advantage-weighted regression:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{wFM}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal D,\ \tau,\ \varepsilon}\Big[\,w(s,a)\,\big\lVert v_\theta(x_\tau,\tau,s)-(\varepsilon-a)\big\rVert^2\Big],\qquad w=\exp(A/\beta)\ \text{or}\ r\in[0,1]. \]

These losses do not require the reverse chain’s likelihood, so we can use ODE or higher-order samplers. The training objectives are still different: NFT uses positive/negative implicit policies, while FPO uses a surrogate ratio. We also need to check whether each method works with classifier-free guidance.

2.4ODE → SDE: why flow policies use it

Flow matching / rectified flow samples with the ODE \(\mathrm dx_\tau=v_\theta(x_\tau,\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau\). Given the current state, the next step is deterministic. Its Dirac transition cannot give us the ordinary stochastic density needed for local PPO. This says nothing about whether the final output has a density. Let \(\tau=1\) denote noise and \(\tau=0\) denote data, so generation runs with \(\mathrm d\tau<0\). With an ideal velocity field / score in continuous time, we can construct a reverse SDE with the same marginals:

\[\mathrm dx_\tau=\left[v_\theta(x_\tau,\tau)-\frac{\sigma_\tau^2}{2}\nabla_x\log p_\tau(x_\tau)\right]\mathrm d\tau+\sigma_\tau\,\mathrm d\bar W_\tau,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\mathrm d\bar W_\tau)=|\mathrm d\tau|I.\]

For rectified flow, \(x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\varepsilon\), and the score is \(\nabla\log p_t(x)=-[x+(1-t)v_\theta(x,t)]/t\). Substitution and Euler–Maruyama discretization give the Flow-GRPO transition below. Here \(t\) is the flow interpolation variable, not environment time:

\[ x_{t+\Delta t}=x_t+\Big[v_\theta(x_t,t)+\frac{\sigma_t^2}{2t}\big(x_t+(1-t)v_\theta(x_t,t)\big)\Big]\Delta t+\sigma_t\sqrt{|\Delta t|}\,\epsilon,\qquad \sigma_t=a\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{1-t}} . \]

Each discrete transition is isotropic Gaussian. With the same diffusion coefficient, its KL is \(\mathrm{KL}=\frac{|\Delta t|}{2}\big(\frac{\sigma_t(1-t)}{2t}+\frac1{\sigma_t}\big)^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert^2\). The hyperparameter \(a\) controls exploration. Generation has \(\Delta t<0\); covariance and KL use \(|\Delta t|\). Finite-step solvers need not exactly preserve continuous-time marginals, and endpoints require appropriate treatment.

ReinFlow instead adds a learned noise network directly to each flow step:

\[ a^{k+1}=a^k+v_\theta(t_k,a^k,s)\,\Delta t_k+\sigma_{\theta'}(t_k,a^k,s)\,\epsilon,\qquad \pi(a^{k+1}\mid a^k,s)=\mathcal N\big(a^k+v_\theta\Delta t_k,\ \operatorname{diag}\sigma_{\theta'}^2\big). \]

This gives us a discrete Markov chain, whose joint likelihood is a product of Gaussian transitions. RL learns the noise scale within upper and lower bounds, and the models discussed here use only 1–4 steps. The price is that we no longer have a guarantee of preserving the pretrained marginals. πRL tries both approaches: Flow-Noise uses learned noise and a single-level formulation; Flow-SDE uses an SDE and a DPPO-like two-level MDP.

2.6General on-policy estimators

PPO + GAE

Schulman et al. 2017 · 1707.06347；GAE 2015 · 1506.02438
\[ \mathcal L^{\text{CLIP}}=\mathbb E\big[\min(\rho_t A_t,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1\pm\epsilon)A_t)\big],\quad \rho_t=\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)},\quad A_t^{\text{GAE}}=\textstyle\sum_l(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l} \]

Train \(V_\phi\) using a value loss, often with value clipping. Policy clipping is a surrogate that discourages large updates, not a hard KL bound. Several epochs reuse one rollout batch. Common implementation choices include normalized advantages and target-KL early stopping.

GRPO

Shao et al. 2024（DeepSeekMath）· 2402.03300
\[ \hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r_{1:G})}{\operatorname{std}(r_{1:G})},\qquad \mathcal J=\mathbb E\Big[\tfrac1G\textstyle\sum_i\tfrac1{|o_i|}\sum_t\min(\rho_{i,t}\hat A_i,\operatorname{clip}(\cdot)\hat A_i)-\beta\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\Big],\quad \mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\approx\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-\log\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-1 \]

GRPO uses the mean of \(G\) samples under the same condition as its baseline, so there is no learned critic. Each sample’s tokens or denoising steps share one outcome advantage. The k3 expression above estimates \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\) on samples from \(\pi_\theta\). If we reuse old-policy samples, we need to account for that change in distribution. In the original GRPO objective, this KL penalty goes directly into the loss.

RLOO / REINFORCE++

Ahmadian et al. 2024 · 2402.14740；Hu et al. 2025 · 2501.03262

RLOO uses \(\hat A_i=r_i-\frac1{G-1}\sum_{j\ne i}r_j\), without dividing by a standard deviation. With independent sibling samples, the leave-one-out baseline does not bias the score-function estimator. REINFORCE++ uses a critic-free, globally normalized batch advantage rather than group normalization, and puts KL into token-level rewards. These estimator choices can be considered separately from the diffusion policy parameterization.

Practical difficulties for diffusion policies

  1. Which likelihood is tractable? Deterministic ODE / DDIM steps do not supply ordinary local stochastic densities; Gaussian stochastic transitions do. The marginal \(\pi(a^0\mid s)\) still generally requires integrating out the chain. Flow policies can use SDEs, injected noise, or alternative objectives (FPO / NFT). GenPO uses a specialized invertible construction with augmented dummy actions to obtain tractable likelihoods.
  2. Credit within denoising. Reward arrives only at \(x_0\). Common choices share that signal (DPPO adds \(\gamma_{\text{denoise}}^k\); GRPO broadcasts), or learn a denoising-state critic, such as DPOK’s optional \(V(x_k,c)\). Step-aware preference/reward methods require additional models or sampling.
  3. Variance. There are \(K\) terms in the score sum. Its variance depends on each term and on their covariances, so it does not simply grow with \(K\). Still, long chains can be hard to optimize. DPPO only trains the last few steps, DanceGRPO samples a subset (about 60% in the setup discussed here), and MixGRPO trains a moving window.
  4. Compute. Each environment decision requires \(K\) model evaluations, and every trained transition adds another forward/backward evaluation. Examples include 10 training steps versus 40 inference steps in Flow-GRPO, five-step DDIM, and 1–4-step shortcut / ReFlow policies in ReinFlow.
  5. Exploration noise. Tiny late-step variance weakens exploration and can make likelihood ratios numerically unstable. Solutions include DPPO variance floors, Flow-GRPO’s noise multiplier \(a\), and ReinFlow’s learned noise. Larger perturbations can also reduce sample quality and move samples away from familiar behavior.
  6. KL to the pretrained model. When the initial-noise distribution is the same, the sum of expected transition KLs upper-bounds the terminal KL. For Gaussians with equal covariance, we can calculate it in closed form. DanceGRPO reports little benefit from KL in its experiments. I would not take that as a reason to drop regularization everywhere: learned rewards can be exploited, so KL, BC, and independent evaluation may still be needed.

3Inside the chain: likelihood ratios on denoising transitions

These methods treat some denoising transitions as RL actions. Those are the solid cells, where we compute log probabilities and ratios. The differences are which steps get updated, how we compute their advantages, and where we put the critic. We use condition \(c\) or \(s_t\), latent \(x_k\), old policy \(\theta_{\mathrm{old}}\), reference \(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}\), advantage \(\hat A\), and ratio \(\rho\) throughout.

Idea

7Image / video versus robotics: what actually corresponds?

After going through the methods, I want to compare image/video generation and robotics more directly. The questions are the same: what do we generate, where do we get reward, which random variable do we compute the ratio on, and how do we assign credit across time? Here I compare the formulations, rather than rank scores from different benchmarks.

Image / video

During one generation, the condition \(c\) is fixed. The output \(x_0\) can be a whole image or video, which gets a score \(R(x_0,c)\) after generation. A video has a time axis, but generating more frames does not by itself add an environment MDP. For that, we need actions, new observations, and further decisions in between.

Robotics

The denoising chain generates an action or action chunk \(A_j\). We execute it and reach \(s_{j+1}\), which conditions the next chain. So the next generation depends on both the previous output and the environment. We need to distinguish three lengths: \(K\) generation steps, \(H\) predicted actions, and \(h\) executed actions.

7.1 One score-function identity, two return problems

Let us first write down the objective for generating and scoring one image or video. If the initial-noise distribution is fixed, only the conditional transitions contribute a gradient. For now, leave out the baseline, PPO clipping, and KL terms:

\[p_\theta(x_{0:K}\mid c)=p(x_K)\prod_{k=1}^{K}\pi_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c),\qquad J_{\mathrm{gen}}=\mathbb E[R(x_0,c)].\] \[\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{gen}}=\mathbb E\!\left[R(x_0,c)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)\right].\]

For a robot, write the final action distribution as \(\pi_\theta(A_j\mid s_j)\). The policy gradient uses the advantage of this decision for future environment return. We can expand the score into denoising-level terms in the implementation. DPPO uses a particular estimate and weighting, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{denoise}}^k A_j\). Simply replacing an image score \(R\) with the immediate environment reward \(r_j\) does not give us the same update.

\[J_{\mathrm{robot}}=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{n\ge0}\gamma_{\mathrm{env}}^n r_n\right],\qquad \nabla_\theta J\;\propto\;\mathbb E_{s_j,A_j}\!\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_j\mid s_j)\,A^\pi(s_j,A_j)\right].\]

The state expectation here uses the visitation distribution for the discounted objective. For generation, we often give all steps in a chain the final sample’s score. For control, we also need to estimate what this action does to future states. This is what the critic helps us with; it is not required just because the task involves a robot.

7.2 Action chunking: define the duration of an environment step

Suppose that at replanning step \(j\), we predict \(H\) actions but only execute the first \(h_j\le H\), without calling the generator again. Let \(n_j\) be the starting primitive control step. Set \(b_j=0\) at true termination and 1 otherwise. If \(\gamma\) is the discount per primitive control step, we get the following chunk reward and bootstrap:

\[\bar r_j=\sum_{u=0}^{h_j-1}\gamma^u r_{n_j+u},\qquad \Gamma_j=\gamma^{h_j},\qquad \delta_j=\bar r_j+\Gamma_j b_j V(s_{j+1})-V(s_j).\] \[\hat A_j=\delta_j+\Gamma_j\lambda b_j\hat A_{j+1}.\]

Here \(\lambda\) is defined per replanning decision. We could instead define the discount per chunk, but that would change the time scale. We also need to distinguish a time-limit truncation from true termination when deciding whether to bootstrap. The remaining \(H-h_j\) actions were never executed, so they contributed no direct environment reward to this chunk. We cannot count predicted actions as executed ones. These equations use one chunk-level convention; an implementation may choose another.

7.3 Why “same-prompt groups” do not become “same-batch groups”

Flow-GRPO / DanceGRPO compare samples generated under the same condition \(c\). In a robot batch, however, \(s_1,s_2,\ldots\) may come from different task stages and difficulty levels. If we normalize their returns together, we mix up the quality of the action with how good the starting state already was.

\[\hat A_i^{\mathrm{gen}}=\frac{R(x_0^{(i)},c)-\bar R_c}{\operatorname{std}_c(R)+\epsilon},\qquad \hat A_i^{Q}(s)=Q(s,a_i)-\frac1N\sum_{m=1}^{N}Q(s,a_m).\]

One option is to restore the same state and sample several future trajectories. Another is to generate several candidate actions at that state and use a critic to estimate their returns. The first needs resets or environment branching. The second depends on the critic, including its error on out-of-distribution actions; OGPO’s same-state chain comparison takes this approach. Also, subtracting a mean and dividing by a standard deviation are different choices. They give different update scales in the two equations above.

7.4 Which log probability is tractable?

We can calculate each Gaussian transition’s density, but this does not automatically give us the final-action marginal. With a saved latent trajectory, we can calculate a joint ratio. For the final-action density, we still have to integrate out the intermediate variables. Clipping each step, clipping the product over the chain, and doing PPO on the final-action marginal give three different objectives.

\[\pi_\theta(a^0\mid s)=\int p(a^K)\prod_{k=1}^{K}\pi_\theta(a^{k-1}\mid a^k,s)\,\mathrm da^{1:K},\qquad \rho_{\mathrm{chain}}=\exp\!\left(\sum_k\Delta\log\pi_k\right).\] \[\sum_k\min(\rho_k A,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_k)A)\;\ne\;\min\!\left((\prod_k\rho_k)A,\operatorname{clip}(\prod_k\rho_k)A\right).\]

This is something to check in the code when reading DPPO, ReinFlow, OGPO, or GenPO. A fixed initial-noise prior cancels in the ratio. If we train the noise policy, as in Noise PPO / DSRL, we are optimizing a different object. FPO’s CFM-loss ratio is a surrogate; it is different from both exact density ratios above.
